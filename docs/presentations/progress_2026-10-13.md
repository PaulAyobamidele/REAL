## 1. Where we were on 24 September

The REAL loop ran end to end on the GPU cluster: a requirement → scenarios in
CARLA with YOLO perception → failure analysis → obstacles → a person reviews →
a proposed new requirement. Two rounds:

- **Round 1** (emergency braking): 91 of 120 real encounters failed (76 %).
- **Round 2** (proportional braking): 4 of 128 failed (3 %), but 113 passes
  were standoffs — the car stopped and never drove on.

The mitigation moved the failure rather than removing it.

## 2. The target and the plan

**ICSE 2027 Tool Demonstrations, submission 23 October 2026**: 4 pages
including references, a 3-5 minute video, and a tool reviewers can run without
building it (`docs/paper/icse27_call.md`).

The plan now has three parts (`docs/paper/study_plan.md`):

| Part | What it is | Needs a GPU |
|---|---|---|
| **The tool** | write any requirement, run it on the cluster, analyse, decide, revise | yes, for running |
| **A pre-computed space** | one requirement, every scenario it can produce, four versions of the car, run once and shipped as a dataset | no — pre-computed |
| **A participant study** | ~10 people review the same pre-computed round and decide independently; each person's revised requirement gets its own follow-up run; before/after compared per person | no for the review; yes for follow-ups |

The study is the paper's evidence that the decision genuinely needs human
judgement; the follow-up runs show what that judgement produced — the loop
closed, with the human step recorded rather than asserted.

## 3. What we built and found, in order

### 3.1 Assumptions about the world are now real (§8.12-8.18)

Rounds 1-2 stated no assumptions, so every failure was blamed on the car by
default. Now every `assuming` item is checked after each simulation — **held,
broken, or not measured** — and a failure counts as *spurious* only if an
assumption was actually broken. Assumptions about the car itself are refused
(otherwise any failure could be defined away). Each assumption gets a verdict
(does breaking it go with more failures?), and the reviewer can keep, tighten,
loosen, drop or add assumptions. Rounds 1-2 re-judged this way: every failure
stays the car's.

### 3.2 Our own test scene was flawed — five fixes (§8.19-8.38)

Short test runs on the cluster exposed one flaw at a time: the pedestrian
waited until the car was 8 m away; one crossing direction never crossed; the
distance setting did nothing; the car could drive off the road; the
pedestrian walked away from the road; then crossed before the car arrived.
Each was fixed and re-checked, ending with a scene where the pedestrian waits
at the kerb and steps out to meet the car, with a timeout. Round 1's apparent
"left-to-right" weakness was partly a scene artefact. A **laptop check** now
builds the real scenes in seconds before any cluster time is used.

### 3.3 Run 2b: round 2's car on the fixed scene (§8.40-8.41, §8.50)

160 simulations, all valid as a test. **158 real encounters, 3 failed (1.9 %)**
— genuine close calls with the pedestrian in the car's lane — but **147 of 155
passes were standoffs**, and 105 runs missed "drive on within 10 s". The car is
safe but freezes: it brakes for anyone it can see.

Checking every failure step by step also exposed three measurement problems,
all fixed on the laptop from the saved traces: simulator glitches inflating
the pedestrian's speed; braking values reflecting the simulator's abrupt stops;
and a safety rule that counted driving past someone on the pavement as a
failure.

### 3.4 The first smart search exploited the rule (§8.43)

The evolutionary search (GE) over 21,120 situations worked — its pass rate fell
generation by generation, and it deliberately went beyond the assumptions — but
everything it "found" was the car passing a pedestrian outside its lane. It had
learned to game the flawed safety rule. A textbook case of a search exploiting
its oracle, and the strongest argument for the next decision.

### 3.5 Decision: the safety rule is path-aware (§8.45, §8.47)

The 5 m margin counts only while the pedestrian is **in the car's lane**. It is
now applied everywhere — in the analysis **and** in the simulator's own
pass/fail check, which is also the search's score — through one shared
function, so the two can never disagree (checked on 200 random traces). Every
report states which rule it used.

### 3.6 More of the world can now vary (§8.46)

New scenario parameters, each recorded in every run, checkable as an
assumption, and usable as a keyword in the requirement's scenario sentence:
**time of day** (sun altitude; below zero is night), **rain**, **wet road**,
**cloud**, and **occlusion** — a parked car on the pedestrian's kerb, between
the car and the crossing. Checked in CARLA: the video shows dusk light, a wet
road with puddles, the parked car, and the pedestrian crossing in front of it.

### 3.7 The car runs exactly what the requirement names (§8.47)

A misspelt or unimplemented braking module or detector used to run silently as
a different car. Now it is **refused**, with the list of what exists. A new
braking module, **path-aware braking**, brakes only for a person the camera
estimates to be in the lane, then drives on — the candidate fix for the
standoff. The estimate uses the camera alone (no simulator ground truth), so a
child is judged further out than they are: a genuine weakness the study can
surface.

### 3.8 First comparison on the new scene (§8.49-8.50)

Four scenes (day; night + wet + rain + parked car; dusk + wet + parked car;
fog past the assumed limit), one trial each — a lead, not a finding:

| | Proportional braking | Path-aware braking |
|---|---|---|
| Day | safe, froze | drove on, braked too late |
| Night, wet, rain, parked car | safe, froze | never braked — 2.1 m |
| Dusk, wet, parked car | braking released | braking released, drove on |
| Fog past the limit | safe, froze | seen but not braked — 1.6 m |

The fix cures the standoff but costs safety, especially at night and in fog —
the "mitigation shifts the failure" trade-off the REAL paper predicts,
measured. One more analysis correction came out of it: a pedestrian stopped in
the lane by an arriving car is now judged as an encounter, not set aside.

### 3.9 Running at scale (§8.47-8.49)

Several cluster jobs can now run at once: each job gets its own network ports
and its own experiment-log store, and a logging failure can no longer stop a
run. This is what the pre-computed space needs.

### 3.10 The tool reviewers and participants use

- A **review interface** (React): the five-step review, a form for writing a
  requirement that only offers what the executor implements, a pattern map of
  failures, and a **live cluster connection** in progress (submit, follow,
  bring results back, the same steps as by hand; the cluster's code copy is
  checked against the laptop's before anything is submitted).
- A **Docker demo**: one command runs the whole loop on real rounds with no
  simulator or GPU.

## 4. How the work is kept honest

- Every result is a file in its run folder; partial or hypothetical results
  say so; superseded reports are moved aside with a note, never deleted.
- Predictions are written down before runs (and checked afterwards).
- Every failure that matters is inspected step by step before it is believed.
- Every code change is reviewed first; 268 automated tests, none needing the
  simulator; the laptop scene check runs before every cluster submission.
- Carbon footprint so far: about 8.4 GPU-hours, at most 5.6 kWh (to be
  recomputed with the final runs).
