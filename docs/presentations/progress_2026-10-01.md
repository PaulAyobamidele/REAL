## 1. Where we were last time (30 s)

At the last presentation we had the REAL loop running end to end on Narval:
a requirement goes in, scenarios are run in CARLA with real YOLO perception,
failures are analysed into obstacles, a human reviews them, and the tool
proposes a revised requirement. Two rounds had run. Round 1 used emergency
braking: 91 of 120 real encounters failed, 76%. Round 2 used proportional
braking: failures fell to 3%, but 113 passes were standoffs, where the car
stopped and never moved on. So the mitigation shifted the failure rather than
solving it.

What I want to show today is what changed since then, what we found, and
what is still open.

---

## 2. What came out of our meeting (1 min)

After the presentation we agreed four things:

1. **Rounds 1 and 2 had no domain assumptions.** The requirement never said
   what we assume about the world. So every failure counted as the car's
   fault by default, and the paper's split between *valid* and *spurious*
   failures never actually ran on real data.
2. The requirement should have a **baseline with both slots filled**:
   assumptions about the world (`assuming`) and soft goals (`ensuring`).
3. After a run, that baseline should be what **decides which failures are
   admissible and which are spurious**.
4. To complete what the paper claims, all three of **D, S and R must be
   adjustable** (domain assumptions, the system, and the requirement), and we
   should consider **a newer YOLO model**.

I also compared the tool point by point with the REAL paper. That's written
up in `docs/design/tool_paper_alignment.md`. The tool already automates things
the paper did by hand, like grouping failures into obstacles, and it records
human decisions. But so far it had only exercised the system layer, never D
or R, never GE in a round, and it measured no smoothness, although the
paper's soft goal is *SmoothBraking*.

---

## 3. Domain assumptions are now real (2 min)

This is the first big piece of work.

The tool now reads each line of the `assuming` clause and sorts it:
something about the scene setup, like fog; something measured in the run,
like how far apart the car and pedestrian start; plain text it can't check;
or something it refuses.

**It refuses any assumption about the car.** If you write "assuming the car
drives slowly", it rejects it. An assumption may describe the world, never the
system under test; otherwise you could define any failure away. That's
exactly the paper's definition of a domain property.

After every simulation, each assumption is marked **held, broken, or not
measured**. A failure is spurious only if something was actually broken.
Something we don't record is never counted as held.

Then, for each assumption, the tool asks whether failures go up when it is
broken. If they do, it's **load-bearing**. If not, it's a candidate for
loosening. If it was never broken, it's **untested**, and the tool flags any
attempt to loosen it as having no evidence.

In the review, every assumption can be kept, tightened, loosened or dropped,
and new ones added. The tool writes those choices into the next requirement
as `[D]` changes.

**The result on rounds 1 and 2.** I wrote the baseline requirement,
`R_baseline.dsl`, and re-judged both rounds against it. Those reports are
saved in a separate folder, clearly labelled "assumptions stated after the
run". Under the baseline, every failure in rounds 1 and 2 **stays a real
violation**: round 1 is still 91 of 120 failed; round 2 is 4 of 126 (it was 4
of 128, because two passing runs that started too close are set aside). None of
them is explained away by a broken assumption. But the rounds also give **no
evidence** for or against any assumption, because none was broken often
enough in real encounters. *(artifacts/runs/…/baseline_D0/, Notes §8.18)*

That second finding is what pushed the next step.

## 4. We found problems in our own test scene (2 min)

To get evidence about assumptions, the scene has to vary in the right ways.
When I read the scene code closely, I found four problems. They matter for
interpreting rounds 1 and 2. *(Notes §8.19)*

1. **The 8-metre trigger.** The pedestrian waited until the car was 8 m away
   before stepping out. At 7.5 m/s the car needs about 8 m to stop. So
   "detected too late" was partly built into the scene, and the round-2
   standoff was partly built in too: a careful car stopped further back, and
   the pedestrian never moved.
2. **"Right-to-left" was not a crossing.** That pedestrian walked along the
   road towards the car. So round 1's finding that left-to-right crossings
   fail 24 points more often is **at least partly a scene artefact**, not a
   perception weakness. We have to say that openly.
3. **The distance setting did nothing.** Long and Short gave the same scene.
4. **The 25% "no-encounter" runs**: the car drove away from the pedestrian
   from the start.

I think this is a good story for the paper, not an embarrassing one. The
tool's own analysis pointed at the patterns that made us look, and REAL has a
label for it: `[T]`, the test itself was wrong.

## 5. The fixed scene, and soft goals we can measure (1 min)

The new scene:
- the pedestrian starts at a kerb and **really crosses, in both directions**
- they step out at once instead of waiting for 8 m
- the car starts 20 or 35 m away depending on the distance setting
- simulations can last 25 s instead of 10, so we can see whether a stopped
  car moves on

Every run now records pedestrian speed, braking smoothness, time-to-collision,
and whether and how fast the car moved on again. Soft goals like
"`resume_within_s <= 10`" are now **checked per run**, met or missed. That
gives the R lever something real to work with.

---

## 6. GE is now the main search (1.5 min)

We decided to use grammatical evolution from here on. That needed three
things.

First, **a grammar worth searching.** The old one had only 32 scenarios, so
GE had nothing to evolve. The new grammar has **21,120**, with numeric fog,
car distance, walking speed and crossing trigger. The ranges deliberately go
beyond the baseline assumptions (thicker fog, closer starts, faster
pedestrians), so GE will produce the "assumption broken" cases that rounds 1
and 2 never had.

Second, **GE runs through the same path as the grid**: launched from a
requirement file on Narval, with full provenance recorded from the start.
Wiring this up turned up two bugs that would have crashed a GE run on the new
grammar, and both are fixed.

Third, **honest analysis.** GE breeds failing scenarios, so its failure rates
are biased upward. Reports on GE runs now say so at the top. They also count
each distinct scenario once next to the raw numbers, and label supported
obstacles as "GE leads, confirm on grid". A small grid check then re-runs
GE's 4 worst and 4 safest scenarios equally often, to confirm or reject those
leads.

## 7. The first smoke run, and what it caught (1 min)

Before any real run, I ran the new scene once per scenario on Narval: 32
simulations, 1 hour 23 minutes. *(artifacts/runs/130e0c8b…/smoke_check.md)*

What worked: no errors, all the new measurements recorded, no-encounter
runs down from about 25% to **1 of 32**, and the distance setting now really
changes the start (20 m versus 35 m).

What failed: when I looked at the video, **the car drove over the kerb** and
stopped on the pavement. The likely cause is that the car was placed facing
against its own lane, and the lane-following controller turned it off the
road. That may also explain the old "drives away" runs. So none of that run's
outcome numbers can be trusted, and I haven't used them.

It's fixed now: everything is placed along the lane's own direction, the
scene is rejected if the car doesn't face the crossing, and every step
records whether the car is on the road. If it leaves, the run is reported as
a test defect, never as a pass. **The second smoke run is queued on Narval
now.**

Two early leads to check once the scene is sound: the car seems to brake as
soon as it sees anyone, about 20 m away; and the scene itself sometimes makes
the pedestrian run faster than 3 m/s.

## 8. How the work is kept honest (30 s)

A few habits run through all of this:
- every result is a file in the run folder, never only a number in a chat
- anything partial or hypothetical says so on the first line
- predictions are written down before a run
- the original reports are never overwritten
- every change to the code is reviewed before it goes in
- there are now 152 automated tests, and none of them needs the simulator
